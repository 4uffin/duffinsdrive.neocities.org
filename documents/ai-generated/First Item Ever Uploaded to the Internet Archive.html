Fact-check · Internet Archive history

What was the first item ever uploaded to the Internet Archive?

The honest answer is that there is no single definitive “first item.” The question splits into at least three different things — a web crawl, a modern archive.org item, and the first item of a named collection — and the Archive’s own metadata does not support a clean “first upload” in 1996.

Research checked 22 November 2026 · Primary sources: archive.org, Internet Archive Blog, Wayback Machine

Bottom line

Don’t say “the first item uploaded to the Internet Archive was X in 1996” — without a qualifier, that claim isn’t supported.

In 1996–2001 the Archive stored web crawls on tape. There was no public archive.org item-upload system. The item system that produces an identifier, an addeddate, and an “Uploaded by …” line only yields a datable first item in 2001.

Which “first” do you mean?

Pick a meaning below. Each one has a different best-supported answer — and a different level of confidence.

Confidence: Medium-high — earliest in the index

Larry King Live, CNN — 14 March 2001, 05:00 UTC

The earliest item in the current archive.org advanced-search index with a credible, corroborated added date. Identifier CNN_20010314_050000_Larry_King_Live — “Uploaded by TV Archive on March 14, 2001,” with date, addeddate and publicdate all agreeing at 2001-03-14T05:00:00Z.

This is not proof that no earlier item ever existed — see the caveats in the detailed section below.

Source: archive.org — item page · Advanced Search index, checked 22 Nov 2026

Confidence: Medium

A BBC page dated 1 March 1995 — imported, not a founding upload

The Wayback Machine’s earliest known page is described as a landing page for the BBC, archived 1 March 1995. But that crawl predates the Archive’s founding and was imported later — it is not a 1996 founding upload.

The first founding-era capture that can be opened today is the Microsoft Internet Explorer download page, 10 May 1996, 14:42:31 UTC, verified from the timestamp embedded in the Wayback URL itself. The BBC date rests on a Wikipedia citation flagged as malformed.

Sources: Wikipedia (Wayback Machine) · Wayback capture of the IE page · Chris R. Cook, “The Oldest Pages in the Wayback Machine,” 25 Oct 2024

Confidence: High — that no named item exists

No item is named — in any official source

No official history, blog post, or interview found names a first item uploaded by Brewster Kahle or the Archive at its founding in 1996. In 1996–2001 the Archive stored web crawls on tape; there was no public item-upload system.

“We started by archiving the most transient of media, which was the World Wide Web’s pages. We did that for five years before we even made the Wayback Machine.”

— Brewster Kahle, 25th-anniversary keynote · Source: Internet Archive Blog, 22 Oct 2021

Confidence: High — stated by the Archive itself

Live Music Archive: Rusted Root, not the Grateful Dead

The first Live Music Archive item was Rusted Root at Alpine Valley, 24 Aug 2001 (identifier rr01-08-24.shnf), uploaded by Jon Aizen on 12 Aug 2002 — stated explicitly by the Internet Archive’s own blog.

For Prelinger, there is no single first item: the earliest items share one bulk ingest date, 16 July 2002.

Source: Internet Archive Blog, 12 Aug 2022 · Rusted Root item page

First, don’t conflate two different systems

A Wayback Machine capture and an archive.org item upload are different kinds of object, from different eras. Most wrong answers online mix them up.

⚠ Caveats — read these before citing it as “the first”

• This is the earliest item in the current advanced-search index with a corroborated added date — not proof no earlier item ever existed.
• Items whose added date was lost or placeholdered (0000-01-01, chiefly etree / Grateful Dead items with public dates 2004–2011) cannot be dated from metadata alone, so an earlier item could in principle hide among them — but none has any evidence of a pre-2001 upload, and the Live Music Archive itself did not start until July–August 2002.
• The CNN items are TV recordings from the TV News Archive feed, not a “public upload” in the modern sense. There is no evidence they were the first thing anyone uploaded — only that they are the earliest surviving item records with a trustworthy creation timestamp.

The runner-up that doesn’t hold up: Democracy Now!, “17 July 2000”

Democracy Now! Monday, July 17, 2000 — debunked as a 2000 upload

dn2000-0717

On paper this item looks earlier: added date 2000-07-17. It is the only item in the index with that added date, and a range search for 18 July 2000 – 13 March 2001 returns zero items.

But its own item page gives it away: “Uploaded by Democracy Now! on May 18, 2018,” public date 2018-05-18T19:49:38Z, scanned with the “Internet Archive HTML5 Uploader 1.6.3” — a modern uploader. The 2000-07-17 added date was evidently backfilled to the broadcast date when the item was (re)created in 2018. Not a 2000 upload.

Source: archive.org — item page for dn2000-0717, checked 22 Nov 2026

Timeline: how the “firsts” actually line up

Every date below is from the research sources listed at the end of this page. Hedges are kept where the sources hedge.

1 MARCH 1995 — CLAIMED

BBC page archived

Described on Wikipedia as the Wayback Machine’s earliest known page, a BBC landing page. The crawl predates the Archive’s founding and was imported later. Its Wikipedia citation is flagged as malformed — treat the exact date as claimed, not verified. Chris Cook’s 2024 research reports March 1995 crawls of bbcnc.org.uk (BBC Networking Club) and 33 pages from 1995 in total.

10 MAY 1996

Internet Archive founded — and the IE capture

Brewster Kahle founded the Archive in San Francisco on 10 May 1996, alongside Bruce Gilliat, around the same time he began Alexa Internet. The Microsoft Internet Explorer download page capture is timestamped this same day at 14:42:31 UTC — verified from the live Wayback URL.

OCTOBER 1996

The Archive’s own first crawl

“In October of 1996, engineers at the San Francisco-based Internet Archive launched their first web crawlers, taking snapshots of web pages. At the time, the World Wide Web was only 2.5 terabytes in size.” The Archive’s 25th-anniversary timeline notes the crawlers captured first web pages “including MTV” — a set of pages, not one named item.

LATE 1999

First expansion beyond the web: Prelinger

The Archive expanded beyond the web archive “beginning with the Prelinger Archives” — the first non-web collection. No single Prelinger film is singled out as first; the earliest items arrive later as a bulk batch (see 16 July 2002).

14 MARCH 2001 · 05:00 UTC

Earliest datable item: Larry King Live on CNN

Identifier CNN_20010314_050000_Larry_King_Live, uploaded by TV Archive. Date, added date and public date all agree — the earliest corroborated item record in the current index.

25 OCTOBER 2001

Wayback Machine opens to the public

Launched for public access with, by then, over 10 billion archived pages already collected. From 1996 to 2001 that data had been kept on digital tape.

16 JULY 2002

Prelinger bulk ingest

The earliest Prelinger items in advanced search share a single added date, 2002-07-16T00:00:00Z — e.g. ToMarket1942_2, StoryofT1956, Berkeley1923. A mass ingest, not a first upload.

12 AUGUST 2002

First Live Music Archive item: Rusted Root

Jon Aizen uploaded Rusted Root’s Alpine Valley recording from 24 Aug 2001 (rr01-08-24.shnf). Aizen had proposed the collection in July 2002 and emailed the etree list on 23 July 2002 offering “unlimited storage, unlimited bandwidth, forever, for free.”

What about 1996 itself?

Official sources name no first item from the founding. The Archive’s own item “The Wayback Machine’s First Crawl 1996” describes crawlers launched in October 1996 by Kahle and Bruce Gilliat — bulk snapshots, not a single upload. Kahle’s founding paper “Preserving the Internet” (4 Nov 1996, for Scientific American, March 1997) likewise names no first item:

“The Internet Archive is such a new organization that is collecting the public materials on the Internet to construct a digital library. The first step is to preserve the contents of this new medium.”

Secondary reporting (IEEE Spectrum; Rosenzweig) agrees crawls began September–October 1996, with Alexa donating bimonthly snapshots, embargoed about six months. Anyone claiming a single 1996 “first upload” is conflating the web crawl with the later item system.

Sources: archive.org — “The Wayback Machine’s First Crawl 1996” · Internet Archive Blog, Kahle keynote, 22 Oct 2021 · Wikipedia (Internet Archive)

The Wayback “first page” claims conflict with each other

BBC, March 1995 (claimed): the Wayback Machine article says the earliest known page is a BBC landing page archived 1 March 1995 — but the footnote is flagged in the article itself as malformed (“|archive-url= is malformed: timestamp”). Cook (2024) independently reports March 1995 crawls of bbcnc.org.uk reaching the Wayback Machine later — and notes they predate both the Archive (May 1996) and Alexa (April 1996), so they cannot be “the first thing Kahle archived at founding.”

USA Today, December 1996 (contradicted): the current Wikipedia Internet Archive article says “The first and oldest page ever archived was a December 1996 edition of USA Today,” citing Rob Pegoraro (USA Today, 29 Oct 2021). An older revision of the same article said instead that the earliest known page was the Internet Explorer download page, saved 10 May 1996 at 14:42 UTC. Since a May 1996 capture verifiably exists, a December 1996 page cannot be the “first and oldest” — unless “first” is meant in some narrower, unstated sense.

MTV (among the earliest, not shown to be first): the Archive’s 25th-anniversary timeline and several histories cite an MTV capture among the earliest 1996 captures, but no single MTV page is shown to be the first.

Sources: Wikipedia (Internet Archive & Wayback Machine), read 22 Nov 2026 · IE capture, 10 May 1996 14:42:31 UTC · Chris R. Cook, 25 Oct 2024

Common claims, checked

Tap any claim to see the evidence. Verdicts follow the primary sources — the Archive’s own blog and item metadata first.

“The first item was a Grateful Dead concert — a rare 1968 recording.”FALSE

The Internet Archive’s own blog (12 Aug 2022) anticipates and rejects this directly:

“It would be a great story to have the first item as part of the collection to be some rare Grateful Dead recording from 1968, but it is actually an unassuming Rusted Root audience recording from August 24, 2001 uploaded to the new Live Music Archive collection by Aizen on August 12, 2002.”

The item is Rusted Root Live at Alpine Valley on 2001-08-24 (rr01-08-24.shnf), East Troy, WI, taped by Jeff Kempka. Track 12, “Ecstasy,” features Mark Karan of Ratdog (Bob Weir’s side project) — the Grateful Dead connection the blog notes. Grateful Dead permission “came a little while later, through Brewster’s connection to John Perry Barlow.” Dead recordings are the cornerstone of the collection today, but they are not its first item — and the collection did not exist before 2002.

Source: Internet Archive Blog, 12 Aug 2022 · Item page

“The first item was a Prelinger film.”PARTLY TRUE

True as a collection: in late 1999 the Archive expanded beyond the web archive “beginning with the Prelinger Archives” — Prelinger is the first non-web collection.

False as a single item: the earliest Prelinger items share one bulk added date, 2002-07-16T00:00:00Z — e.g. ToMarket1942_2 (“To Market, To Market (Part II)”, 1942), StoryofT1956, Berkeley1923. That is a mass ingest, not a first upload, and no Prelinger item is singled out in any source as the first.

Source: Wikipedia (Internet Archive) · archive.org Advanced Search, collection:prelinger sorted by added date, checked 22 Nov 2026

“The first thing archived was an Alexa crawl — Alexa donated the first crawls.”TRUE — IN SUBSTANCE

Alexa Internet was founded in April 1996 by Kahle (with Bruce Gilliat) and donated its crawl data to the Internet Archive from that year. IEEE Spectrum describes Kahle trawling the web with a crawler developed for Alexa, with Alexa donating its twice-yearly global web crawls.

The early web archive is largely Alexa crawl data, donated in bulk and embargoed ~6 months — but there is no identifiable “first Alexa page” in public sources. True in substance; not a single item.

Source: Timeline of digital preservation · IEEE Spectrum, “A Memory of Webs Past” (secondary reporting in research, 22 Nov 2026)

“The first items were early texts / Project Gutenberg books.”UNSUPPORTED

Project Gutenberg (founded 1971 by Michael Hart) is the oldest digital library and a long-standing donor of public-domain books to the Archive — but no source names a Gutenberg text as the first archive.org item, and the item index shows no 1990s added dates at all. The earliest content dates on archive.org are 19th-century and earlier — that is publication date, not upload date. The Archive’s book-scanning programme itself postdates the web archive by years.

Source: archive.org Advanced Search — addeddate:[1990-01-01 TO 1999-12-31] → 0 results, checked 22 Nov 2026

“The first / oldest page was a December 1996 USA Today.”CONTRADICTED

This is the current wording of Wikipedia’s Internet Archive article, citing Pegoraro (USA Today, 29 Oct 2021). It conflicts with (a) the same article’s older wording (IE page, 10 May 1996, 14:42 UTC), (b) the live Wayback URL for that IE page, and (c) the Wayback Machine article’s BBC March 1995 claim. Treat it as a Wikipedia edit, not a primary-source fact.

Source: Wikipedia (Internet Archive), read 22 Nov 2026 · IE capture

“The oldest Wayback pages are from May 1996 — the Archive began archiving in May 1996.”MISLEADING

The Archive was founded 10 May 1996, but its own first crawl was October 1996, and the Wayback index contains imported 1995 crawls (BBC Networking Club, March 1995) that predate the organisation. May 1996 is the founding date — not the date of the oldest capture.

Source: archive.org — “The Wayback Machine’s First Crawl 1996” · Wikipedia (Wayback Machine)

How this was checked — and why the obvious search misleads

The Archive has three different date fields, and only one of them means “when the item was created.” Sorting by the wrong one produces confident, wrong answers.

What the fields actually mean

From the Internet Archive’s own metadata schema:

  • addeddate — for dates before Dec 2019: “Date and time in UTC that the item was created [on] archive.org,” automatically set when the item directory is created in the file system. In some cases a directory is created with metadata but no media files before scanning, so addeddate reflects creation regardless of when media was added. (From Dec 2019 onward it instead marks when the item was added to the public search engine.)
  • publicdate — “The date and time in UTC that the item was made public on archive.org,” set when the derive task finishes.
  • date — the content’s publication date, not the upload date.

Source: archive.org — Metadata Schema

Three naive sorts, three false “firsts” (tested live, 22 Nov 2026)

  • Sort by addeddate ascending: the first 10 hits all show addeddate: 0000-01-01T00:00:00Z — e.g. Grateful Dead gd1966-03-12.sbd.bershaw.9516.shnf (content date 1966-03-12, public date 2005-05-02). 0000-01-01 is a placeholder for migrated etree items, not an upload date.
  • Sort by publicdate ascending: first hits show public date 1995-01-01 but added dates in 2019 — e.g. RokGazet_1995_n07_katalogo. There, publicdate is the content’s publication year, backfilled.
  • Sort by date ascending: worst of all — date is content date, so 19th-century books and 1893 films would “win.”

The range queries that narrow it down

  • addeddate:[1990-01-01 TO 1999-12-31] → 0 items. Nothing in the index claims a 1990s added date.
  • addeddate:[2000-07-17 TO 2000-07-17] → 1 item: only dn2000-0717 (later shown to be backfilled — see above).
  • addeddate:[2000-07-18 TO 2001-03-13] → 0 items. An empty gap of nearly eight months.
  • addeddate:[2001-03-14 TO 2001-03-14] → 2 items: the two Larry King Live editions, 05:00 and 08:00 UTC.
  • One more dead end: numeric_id looks tantalisingly sequential (Rusted Root has Numeric_id 25; Prelinger’s Berkeley1923 has 173), but the API rejects sorting by it — “[UNSUPPORTED_SORT] Cannot sort with specified field (numeric_id)” — and low numbers are scattered across 2002 added dates, so it cannot order items.

Source: archive.org Advanced Search JSON API (advancedsearch.php, sort by addeddate asc), queried 22 Nov 2026 — base query

What could not be verified

An honest fact-check ends with its gaps. These four remain open:

  1. The BBC 1 March 1995 exact capture. The Wikipedia citation is flagged malformed in the article itself, and direct CDX queries to the Wayback CDX server for bbcnc.org.uk, the IE page, and usatoday.com returned HTTP 403 (access denied) on 22 Nov 2026; those requests were not repeated or bypassed. The March 1995 date therefore rests on Wikipedia plus Cook’s 2024 blog, which reports March 1995 crawls of bbcnc.org.uk without giving a 1 March timestamp or a direct Wayback URL in the text fetched. A live-browser CDX check would be needed to confirm the exact timestamp and URL.
  2. The USA Today December 1996 capture. No direct Wayback URL or timestamp was found in the sources fetched; the claim rests solely on the current Wikipedia wording and its Pegoraro citation. The IE May 1996 capture contradicts it as “first/oldest,” but the specific USA Today capture itself was not opened.
  3. Whether any pre-2001 item record was deleted or lost. The 0000-01-01 placeholders (chiefly etree items) mean the index cannot prove a negative. The CNN March 2001 item is the earliest datable item — not a proven absolute first upload.
  4. Any official “first item” statement. No official Internet Archive statement naming a first item was found in the IA Blog, About pages, Kahle’s 2021 keynote, or the “First Crawl” item description. If the Archive has ever published such a statement, the searches run did not surface it.
